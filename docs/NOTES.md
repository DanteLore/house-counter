## Does Thatcham need more housing?

*Written from a data-driven planner's perspective. Conclusions derived from Land Registry PPD, OS Open UPRN, ONS CPI, and Census data — not from any pre-existing planning target.*

### Headline conclusions

1. **West Berkshire needs more housing.** Affordability ratio ~10× (England: 7.7×), owner-occupancy falling, private renting rising, district under-delivery vs standard method for a decade.

2. **Thatcham's prices remain persistently below the district average and are not catching up.** The Thatcham-to-West-Berkshire median ratio was 90% in 2001, fell to 83% by 2010, and has only recovered to 90% in 2025 — no net gain in 25 years. Buyers in an expensive district are not bidding Thatcham up relative to its neighbours. Turnover tracks the national average. Within-type prices confirm this: terraced at district parity, semi and flat ~12–13% below, detached ~20% below.

3. **The one Thatcham-specific finding is 15 years of near-zero delivery.** Population +11.6% (2001–2021); net new general dwellings since 2010: ~150, almost all retirement flats. Every other comparable West Berks settlement kept building.

4. **The case for allocating growth to Thatcham is a siting argument, not a demand argument:**
   - District apportionment — the homes have to go somewhere.
   - Catch-up — 15-year delivery freeze created a 600–800 home shortfall against Thatcham's natural share.
   - Transport — direct electrified rail to Reading (12–15 min), the regional employment cluster that *is* growing post-Elizabeth Line. Local employers (Vodafone, AWE) are stable or contracting.
   - Path of least resistance — AONB rules out west/south, DEPZ rules out east, floodplain limits river corridors, Newbury is at infill saturation. Thatcham's northern edge is the only unconstrained corridor left.

5. **Defensible quantum: 1,400–2,200 homes over 15 years.** Above that is a strategic/capacity judgement, not a needs derivation.

6. **Mix: 60–70% should be 2–4 bed houses (terraced + semi), 30%+ affordable weighted to social rent.** Not more retirement flats. Not more detached-heavy estates.

## 1. Price Paid data (2001–2025)

*Source: Land Registry PPD, OS Open UPRN, ONS CPI. Excludes property type O and latest year (registration lag).*

### Address stock and turnover

| Area | Addresses | Sales 2001–2025 | Avg turnover/yr |
|------|-----------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Newbury polygon | 25,106 | 20,936 | 3.34% |
| Thatcham polygon | 12,250 | 12,232 | 3.99% |
| West Berkshire | ~101,882 | ~64,000 | ~2.5% |

Thatcham's higher turnover is a new-build counting artefact from the 2000s build-out, not elevated resale churn — see §Turnover below.

### Median prices and real growth

| Area | 1995 median | 2025 median | Real CAGR | vs W. Berks |
|------|-------------|-------------|-----------|-------------|
| Newbury | £65,000 | £350,000 | +1.62%/yr | −13% |
| Thatcham | £65,000 | £360,000 | +1.56%/yr | −10% |
| West Berks | £74,000 | £400,500 | — | 100% |

Thatcham's real CAGR (+1.56%/yr) is ~0.26%/yr above England (+1.3%/yr), compounding to ~9pp over 24 years. But the relative-to-district index shows only +2.3pp movement over 30 years (94.7 in 2010 → 102.3 in 2025). Thatcham is marginally ahead of the national average but not diverging from West Berkshire. Year-to-year medians are also vulnerable to mix shifts (new-build phases change the composition around the median).

### Property mix (2020–2025 average, % of all sales)

| Type | Thatcham | Newbury |
|------|----------|---------|
| Detached | 24% | 19% |
| Semi-detached | 36% | 23% |
| Terraced | 25% | 24% |
| Flat | 15% | 34% |

Thatcham is ~85% houses. Newbury is 1-in-3 flats — a direct result of the Racecourse development, Park Way, and Hambridge Lane conversions.

### New-build delivery windows

**Thatcham:**

| Window | New-build sales | Mix |
|--------|----------------|-----|
| 2001–2003 | ~270 | Detached + flat (Dunstan Park tail) |
| 2004–2008 | ~613 | Mixed — Kennet Heath |
| 2009–2016 | ~95 (~12/yr) | Very thin — scattered windfalls |
| 2017–2020 | ~173 | Flat-heavy — retirement/care |
| 2021–2025 | ~92 | **100% flats since 2022** |

Since 2022, every recorded new-build sale in the Thatcham polygon has been a flat. This is a supply suppression finding (no family housing has been permitted and built) — it cannot be used as a demand-side signal.

**Newbury:**

| Window | New-build sales | Mix |
|--------|----------------|-----|
| 2001–2007 | ~1,485 | High volume — Greenham, town-centre flats |
| 2008–2012 | ~263 | Low volume, 60–80% flats |
| 2013–2018 | ~993 | Racecourse Phase 1 — peak flat delivery |
| 2019–2025 | ~500 | Racecourse tail, still flat-dominated |

### Turnover

National average: 3.4%/yr since 2015. Thatcham: 3.4%. Newbury: 3.2%. Thatcham hit 6%+ in 1999–2007 — this coincided exactly with Dunstan Park and Kennet Heath completions. Strip out new-build sales:

| Year | Total | New-build | Resales | Resale turnover |
|------|-------|-----------|---------|-----------------|
| 2004 | 775 | 192 | 583 | 4.8% |
| 2007 | 695 | 103 | 592 | 4.8% |
| 2015 | 467 | 12 | 455 | 3.7% |

**Turnover is not a demand signal.** It is life-cycle churn plus new-build counting. The 2000s peak is entirely explained by completions. Post-2010, Thatcham sits at the national average.

Prices vs incomes is a better signal — but the doubling of the affordability ratio (5× in 1999 → 10× in 2024) is a national phenomenon driven by interest-rate compression and mortgage-lending evolution. England went from 4× to 7.7× over the same period. This is not Thatcham-specific.

### Within-type prices vs district (2025)

| Type | Thatcham | Newbury | W. Berks | Thatcham vs district |
|------|----------|---------|----------|----------------------|
| Detached | £505,000 (85) | £591,250 (130) | £630,000 (657) | **−20%** |
| Semi-detached | £353,750 (142) | £410,250 (150) | £405,000 (658) | −13% |
| Terraced | £330,000 (97) | £345,000 (160) | £336,500 (472) | **−2% (parity)** |
| Flat | £190,250 (36) | £215,000 (226) | £215,000 (352) | −12% |

The 10% headline discount is a mix-weighted average. On a like-for-like basis: terraced is at parity; the only substantial discount is at the top of the market (detached −20%).

The detached gap is plausibly explained by product typology — Thatcham's stock is 1990s/2000s estate-built 3–4 bed, while the district average is pulled up by AONB-village prestige homes. But without a hedonic comparison (same age, same size, different location), this remains a hypothesis rather than a proven finding.

**Developer economics implication:** new detached in Thatcham clears at ~£500–550k vs ~£700k+ in rural West Berkshire. Tighter per-plot margin → weaker council negotiating position on S106 affordable percentage. The same numbers that suppress the council's leverage are attractive to volume housebuilders: reliable sales, predictable margins, minimal planning risk. The structural incentive is toward too many 4-bed detached (highest per-unit revenue) and shared-ownership affordable (lowest developer subsidy) rather than 2-bed terraced and social rent.

**Council levers to correct the mix:**
1. Specify type/tenure split in the allocation policy and defend it through viability challenge.
2. Allocate to a Registered Provider rather than a speculative builder for the affordable-led parcels.
3. Community Land Trust for a portion — acquires land at existing-use value, permanently affordable. Compton precedent exists in the district.
4. Pre-permission CPO under LURA 2023 to capture land value before planning inflates the benchmark. Politically slow but legally available.

Relying on S106 top-ups alone will reproduce the past pattern: ~30% affordable on paper, weighted to shared ownership, wrong product mix.

## 2. Demographics, affordability and jobs

### Population

- West Berkshire: 153,800 (2011) → 161,400 (2021), +5.0%. Below South East (+7.5%) and England (+6.6%).
- Thatcham parish: 22,824 (2001) → 25,464 (2021), **+11.6%** — faster than the district on a static dwelling stock.
- Growth absorbed through densification and inward migration, not new supply.

### Households

- West Berkshire 2021: 66,658 households. 27% single-person.
- Owner-occupancy: 69.7% → 67.6% (2011–2021). Private rented +3.4pp — faster than regional average.

### Affordability

- England median price ÷ earnings: **7.7×** (ONS 2024).
- West Berkshire: £400,500 median vs ~£40k median earnings = **~10×**.
- First-time buyer average: £316k. Average rent: £1,273/month (+2.6% YoY).
- Ratio above 5× = "seriously unaffordable" (Demographia). West Berkshire has been above this for 25+ years.

### Local employers — stable, not growing

| Employer | Jobs at site | Trend |
|----------|-------------|-------|
| Vodafone UK HQ, Newbury | ~4,128 | Contracting — 11,000 global cuts announced May 2023. "+600 to Newbury" was a 2019 internal relocation from Bracknell, not net new jobs. |
| The Connection (ex-Vodafone, 38 acres) | TBC | Oval Real Estate redeveloping as life-sciences campus. No committed occupiers yet. |
| AWE Aldermaston/Burghfield | ~9,500 combined | Stable. 400–500 redundancies 2024–25 replaced by new hire per MOD. Capital investment but flat headcount. |

The West Berkshire labour market is mature and stable. The housing demand case is **not** "local jobs are growing." It is that **Thatcham has direct electrified rail to Reading** — 12–15 minutes, connecting to the Elizabeth Line. Reading has ~120k jobs in tech and financial services, house prices +33% post-Elizabeth Line vs +22% regional average, rents +25% in a year. Thatcham is a natural overflow market for Reading-priced-out buyers.

### Transport

- Electrified GWR line (since 2018). Station entries+exits: 0.465m in 2024/25 (up from 0.107m in 2020/21 — steepest post-COVID rebound of any local station).
- Reading: 12–15 min. Paddington via fast GWR: ~25 min. Thatcham → central London: ~55–70 min.
- A4 through town. M4 J13 (Chieveley) 3 miles north. A34 (Oxford/Southampton) at J13.

Thatcham is as well rail-connected as Newbury (same line). It scores higher than Lambourn, Hungerford, Pangbourne and the rural sub-areas on both rail and road.

### The constraint map — why Thatcham keeps getting picked

| Constraint | What it rules out |
|------------|-------------------|
| North Wessex Downs AONB (south and west) | Hungerford, Lambourn, Compton, Kintbury, Hermitage, rural west |
| AWE DEPZ (3km radius, east) | Tadley, Aldermaston, Mortimer, Burghfield Common |
| Kennet & Lambourn floodplain | Southern edges of Newbury and Thatcham |
| Greenbelt / SSSI (east) | Pangbourne, Theale corridor |
| Newbury infill saturation | Racecourse, Park Way, Hambridge Lane already delivered; Sandleford absorbs southern edge |

What remains: **Thatcham's northern/north-eastern hinterland** — flat agricultural land outside AONB, DEPZ and (just) floodplain, with rail and A4 already in place.

Thatcham keeps receiving allocations not because it has the strongest positive case but because everywhere else has a stronger case against. This is the mechanism that has made Thatcham a dormitory town — 30 years of residential growth steered onto its northern edge without matching expansion of employment, schools or town-centre function.

**The dormitory pattern is not an argument against building.** Thatcham's rail link to Reading *is* the economic justification for building there — it will always be a commuter town and that is fine. The problem with past allocations is not that residents commuted, but that estates were delivered without the schools, transport infrastructure and civic capacity that a commuter town of 25,500 people requires (Dunstan Park: school plot lost; Kennet Heath: rail bridge replaced by roundabout payment). Any new allocation must front-load infrastructure, not bolt it on later.

## 4. What the trends show

### No Thatcham-specific demand signal

| Expected signal | Actual data |
|----------------|-------------|
| Real prices outgrowing national average | Thatcham +1.56%/yr vs England +1.3%/yr — ~9pp gap over 24 years. Real but modest; not confirmed by relative-to-district index (+2.3pp in 30 years). |
| Affordability worsening faster than district | Tracks district, tracks region. The 5×→10× doubling is national. |
| Premium vs district rising | Index: 100 (1995) → 94.7 (2010) → 102.3 (2025). Flat trend with a shallow V. |
| Within-type gaps narrowing | Terraced at parity. Semi −13%, flat −12%, detached −20%. All stable. |
| Turnover above national rate | Resale turnover at national average since 2010. Historic peak is new-build accounting. |
| New-build clearing at premium | Zero family new-build since 2022 — supply suppression, not a demand reading. |

**Conclusion: Thatcham is a normally-functioning part of the regional housing market. It feels the same regional pressure as the rest of the South East. It is not under measurably greater pressure than West Berkshire as a whole.**

### What IS Thatcham-specific

One finding only: **15 years of near-zero family housing delivery against +11.6% population growth.** Under-delivery absorbed silently through densification and displacement — no demand spike shows up in the transaction data because no transactions were generated. This is a delivery failure, not a demand story.

### District picture

| Trend | Local? | Implication |
|-------|--------|-------------|
| Affordability ratio ~10× | No — national | District-wide supply shortfall |
| Standard-method need ~1,070/yr; delivery well below | District | Homes needed somewhere |
| Newbury flat-led model saturated | Newbury | Next strategic site must be elsewhere |
| Mix in recent delivery wrong for demographic | District | 100% retirement flats in Thatcham; 27% single-person households district-wide need family homes |
| Infrastructure lagged past cycles | Local | Warning for next allocation, not argument against building |

## 5. Conclusions

### Does Thatcham need a strategic allocation?

**(a) Is there a Thatcham-specific demand case?** No. The price and transaction data do not pick Thatcham out. The CAGR gap is real but modest and not confirmed by the relative-to-district index.

**(b) Should Thatcham take district growth?** Yes — but as a siting decision, not a demand response. The case rests on: district-level need, 15-year catch-up, rail access to Reading, and the elimination of alternatives by constraint geography.

### How many?

**Tier 1 — what local evidence supports: 1,400–2,200 homes over 15 years.**

Explicit arithmetic:

- District standard-method need: 1,070/yr × 15 years = 16,050 homes. Thatcham share (15.8% of district population): **~2,536**.
- District adopted-plan rate: 515/yr × 15 years × 15.8% = **~1,221**.
- Mid-range of these two baselines: ~1,400–1,900.
- Catch-up: natural delivery ~60–80/yr; actual ~10/yr; shortfall over 15 years = 750–1,050; recoverable portion: **~600–800**.
- Combined: **1,400–2,200**, spanning adopted-plan to standard-method district targets.
- This is the same order of magnitude as the 1995–2008 build-out (Dunstan Park + Kennet Heath + Pound Lane ≈ ~1,200 homes).

**Tier 2 — anything above 2,200** is a strategic/capacity judgement: Thatcham absorbs more than its share because alternatives are worse. Legitimate, but must be labelled as such — not presented as a local-needs finding.

### What type?

| Type | Recommended share | Reason |
|------|-------------------|--------|
| 2–3 bed terraced & small semi | 40–45% | Closes affordability gap; matches town character; highest unmet need |
| 3–4 bed semi-detached | 25–30% | Thatcham's historic strength; undersupplied since 2010 |
| 4–5 bed detached | 10–15% | Already well-served at 24% of stock; widening affordability gap if overbuilt |
| Flats (affordable/shared ownership only) | 10–15% | Market-rate flats saturated; justified only as social rent, shared ownership or key-worker |
| Over-55 / specialist | 5–10% | Demographic need exists but Thatcham has just absorbed a wave; limit pace |

**Tenure: minimum 30% affordable, weighted to social rent not shared ownership.**

### Where?

North/north-east of the existing town — away from floodplain (south), AONB (south-west) and A4 air-quality corridor. Any scheme must include: new primary school (not a re-allocated plot), secondary expansion, rail crossing (the Kennet Heath promise never delivered), SuDS for culverted springs, A4 bus priority and cycle network to station.
